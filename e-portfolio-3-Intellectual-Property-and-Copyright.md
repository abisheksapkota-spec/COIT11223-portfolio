## Artefact 1: Can You Copyright AI-Generated Work?

Source: [Here's What You Can And Can't Copyright With AI, Matt Wolfe, YouTube](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=1A74lC0tHH0)

Wolfe (2025) talks about the US Copyright Office's viewpoint on the situation with copyrighted works produced by AI. This points out the idea that the determining factor in terms of copyrighting something is the presence of human creativity rather than the use of AI in production. An example of this fact can be exemplified by the fact that, by giving a text command with no additional involvement, one does not own the resulting text, for AI is the creator of the piece; however, if someone decides to change the order of the creation or significantly edit the piece that was originally produced, such human engagement can give them copyright rights. I wanted to use this video in my assignment because it helped me answer the burning question that I had before participating in the workshop: who owns the works produced by AI?

## Artefact 2: Anthropic's $1.5 Billion Copyright Settlement

Source: [Anthropic to pay $1.5 billion copyright settlement to authors, publishers, Courthouse News Service, 2026](https://www.courthousenews.com/anthropic-to-pay-1-5-billion-copyright-settlement-to-authors-publishers/)

According to Nairn (2026), the final approval has been granted to Anthropic for a payment of $1.5 billion to publishers and authors, resulting in history's largest copyright class action settlement. Claude, Anthropic's AI (artificial intelligence), was trained on more than 480,000 books obtained illegally. Each author gets about $3,000 in damages for their book.  Previously, the court had determined that training Claude was a fair use, and the payment was related to the illegal possession of pirated copies of books. I selected the article because it demonstrates an example where the company loses heavily due to how information has been acquired, notwithstanding the fact that the training itself was legal. This brings out the workshop's message that copyright is to balance the interests of creators and people who can reuse the copyright material.

## Artefact 3: Getty Images v. Stability AI

Source: [UK High Court: landmark decision on copyright and Gen AI, Shaw & Thornton, Hogan Lovells, 2025](https://www.hoganlovells.com/en/publications/getty-loses-uk-copyright-case-against-stability-ai-)

In the case Getty Images v. Stability AI, the High Court in England ruled that Stability AI's Stable Diffusion algorithm does not represent an "infringing copy" in accordance with UK law, as it did not save a copy of Getty's pictures in any way, which failed in the copyright claim (Shaw & Thornton 2025). However, the Court deemed that Stability is liable for some trademark infringements, since in the early versions of the system outputs it could reproduce the watermark used by Getty. I chose the case as it shows how difficult it is to apply a traditional notion of "copying" to the internal workings of an AI system. Even though they lost the main case regarding copyright, Stability AI was far from being off the hook, since another lawsuit regarding trademark infringement is still pending.
